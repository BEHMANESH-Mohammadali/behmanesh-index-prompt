GLOSSARY.md

Behmanesh Index (BSI)

A structured epistemic evaluation framework designed to assess the robustness of knowledge artifacts through multi-layer analysis.

BSI does not evaluate popularity, authority, consensus, persuasion, or social acceptance.

BSI evaluates Epistemic Robustness.

---

Epistemic Robustness (ER)

The primary construct measured by BSI.

Definition:

The degree to which a knowledge artifact maintains structural coherence, evidential grounding, mechanistic depth, explanatory integrity, causal consistency, and longitudinal stability under multi-layer epistemic analysis.

Epistemic Robustness does not imply truth, scientific consensus, predictive success, or correctness of conclusions.

---

Knowledge Artifact

Any unit of analyzable knowledge.

Examples:

- Scientific papers
- Articles
- Books
- Essays
- Reports
- Arguments
- Research claims
- Policy proposals
- Public statements
- Theoretical frameworks

---

Claim

A proposition presented as knowledge, belief, explanation, prediction, interpretation, or conclusion.

Claims may be explicit or implicit.

---

Evidence

Information used to support, justify, or strengthen a claim.

Evidence may be empirical, observational, statistical, historical, logical, or theoretical.

---

Evidence Quality

The degree to which evidence is reliable, relevant, traceable, and appropriate for supporting a claim.

---

Mechanistic Depth

The extent to which a knowledge artifact explains underlying mechanisms rather than merely describing outcomes.

Mechanistic explanations answer:

"How does this happen?"

---

Causal Structure

The network of cause-effect relationships proposed within a knowledge artifact.

---

Causal Consistency

The degree to which causal explanations remain internally coherent and non-contradictory.

---

Predictive Depth

The degree to which a knowledge artifact generates meaningful expectations, implications, scenarios, or future observations derived from its internal structure.

Predictive Depth does not require successful prediction.

It evaluates predictive architecture.

---

Explanatory Depth

The degree to which a knowledge artifact provides coherent and layered explanations beyond surface-level description.

---

Structural Coherence

The degree to which concepts, claims, evidence, mechanisms, and conclusions form an internally consistent structure.

---

Longitudinal Coherence

The degree to which claims remain coherent across time, contexts, scales, and analytical layers.

---

Multi-Layer Analysis

A BSI principle requiring evaluation across multiple epistemic layers rather than isolated statements.

Typical layers may include:

- Claim Layer
- Evidence Layer
- Mechanism Layer
- System Layer
- Temporal Layer
- Meta-Layer

---

Epistemic Gap

A disconnect between components of a knowledge structure that weakens epistemic robustness.

---

Epistemic Integrity Gap (EIG)

A detected structural weakness in the relationship between claims, evidence, mechanisms, methods, or conclusions.

EIG is a diagnostic subsystem of BSI.

---

Claim-Evidence Gap

An epistemic gap in which claims exceed available supporting evidence.

---

Method-Conclusion Gap

An epistemic gap in which conclusions exceed what the employed methodology can justify.

---

Framing-Content Gap

An epistemic gap in which presentation, framing, or rhetoric implies stronger support than the actual content provides.

---

Longitudinal Consistency Gap

An epistemic gap in which claims become inconsistent across temporal, contextual, or analytical perspectives.

---

Confidence Calibration

The alignment between confidence expressed in a claim and the strength of supporting evidence.

---

Overconfidence

A condition in which confidence exceeds evidential support.

---

Underconfidence

A condition in which confidence is lower than evidential support would justify.

---

Epistemic Humility

The practice of maintaining confidence proportional to available evidence and uncertainty.

---

Interpretive Risk

The probability that a claim, conclusion, or analysis may be distorted by unsupported assumptions, ambiguity, or insufficient evidence.

---

Assumption

A proposition accepted without explicit demonstration within the analyzed artifact.

---

Hidden Assumption

An unstated assumption required for an argument or explanation to function.

---

Inference

A conclusion derived from premises, evidence, or reasoning processes.

---

Reasoning Chain

The sequence of inferential steps connecting evidence and conclusions.

---

Robust Explanation

An explanation that remains coherent when examined across multiple layers, contexts, and analytical perspectives.

---

Ontology

The foundational description of what entities, relationships, and structures exist within the BSI framework.

Ontology answers:

"What exists?"

---

Methodology

The procedural framework describing how BSI performs evaluation.

Methodology answers:

"How should evaluation be conducted?"

---

CORE_BEHMANESH

The foundational reasoning architecture governing evaluation behavior within BSI.

CORE_BEHMANESH defines evaluation principles, constraints, and execution logic.

---

Operationalization

The process of transforming conceptual constructs into observable and evaluable dimensions.

---

Dimension

A measurable component used to operationalize Epistemic Robustness.

Dimensions collectively contribute to the final BSI evaluation.

---

Score

A numerical estimation generated by the BSI evaluation process.

Scores are indicators of epistemic robustness and are not direct measures of truth.

---

Truth

BSI does not directly measure truth.

A claim may possess:

- High BSI and be false.
- Low BSI and be true.

BSI evaluates epistemic robustness rather than objective truth.

---

Scientific Consensus

BSI does not treat consensus as proof of truth.

Consensus may be considered contextual evidence but is not itself a validity criterion.

---

Epistemic Evaluation

The process of assessing the robustness, coherence, support structure, and explanatory quality of a knowledge artifact.

---

First Principle of BSI

Evaluate the robustness of knowledge architecture, not the correctness of conclusions.
